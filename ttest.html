T-Test: Writing Speed

A classroom comparison of writing speed, hand preference, and the script-writing question.

  • 9 students
  • 30-second writing task
  • Three t-tests

The study

What We Did

Our class counted how many times each student could write “I am amazing” in 30 seconds using their writing hand and their other hand. We also recorded each student's answer to the question about whether they write in script. We compared the class's writing-hand mean with Dr. Jacques's benchmark count of 8. We used t-tests to compare the benchmark, the yes and no groups, and each student's two hand counts.

The numbers

My Results

Each count represents how many times a student wrote the phrase during the timed task.

Class results across the three t-tests
Test Mean 1 Mean 2 Difference p-value
One-sample (writing hand vs. benchmark) 6.333 8.000 −1.667 0.00105
Independent-samples (Yes vs. No; Welch) 6.400 6.250 0.150 0.84808
Paired-samples (writing hand vs. other hand) 6.333 2.556 3.778 0.00000647

What the tests suggest

The Three Write-Ups

  1. The class writing-hand mean was 1.667 counts below the benchmark. The result gives strong evidence that the class mean differed from the benchmark, in the lower direction.

  2. The mean for students who answered yes was 0.150 counts higher than the mean for students who answered no. This is a small observed difference, and the result gives little evidence of a group difference.

  3. Students averaged 3.778 more counts with their writing hand than with their other hand. The result gives strong evidence of a difference in the direction of higher writing-hand counts.

The takeaway

In Plain Words

As a group, students wrote the phrase more often with their writing hand than with their other hand. Their writing-hand average was below the professor's benchmark, while the yes and no groups had very similar averages.

Looking ahead

What I Would Do Differently

This was a small study with only nine students, so the results may not represent other students. The professor's benchmark came from one person, and a single timed count may not capture how someone typically writes. I would include more participants and repeat the timed task to get a more reliable comparison.

Practice

Which Test Would You Use?

For each scenario, I picked the test I'd use and wrote down the clue that gave it away.

  1. Blood pressure before and after a six-week program

    Test: Paired t-test.
    The clue: It's the same patients measured twice, once before and once after. Each person gets compared with themselves.

  2. Sleep hours: commuters vs. on-campus students

    Test: Independent (two-sample) t-test.
    The clue: These are two separate groups of students, and nobody is in both. We're comparing the average for one group with the average for the other.

  3. County birth weight vs. the national average of 7.5 lb

    Test: One-sample t-test.
    The clue: There's only one group here. Its average gets checked against a set number, the 7.5 lb national figure, instead of against another group.

  4. Smoking and mental distress across 168 ZIP codes

    Test: Correlation or regression (r, slope and p-value), not a t-test.
    The clue: Nobody is being split into groups. The question is whether two continuous measures go up together across the same ZIP codes. Even a strong result would only show that they're linked, not that smoking causes distress.

  5. Resting heart rate in January and again in June

    Test: Paired t-test.
    The clue: Each member is measured twice, so the January and June numbers belong to the same person and have to stay matched up.

  6. ER wait times at two different hospitals

    Test: Independent (two-sample) t-test.
    The clue: Two hospitals, two different sets of patients. We're comparing one hospital's average wait with the other's.

  7. Health insurance (yes/no) and smoking (yes/no)

    Test: Nothing we've learned yet.
    The clue: Both variables are yes-or-no answers. There's no average to compare and no continuous measure to correlate.

The Odd One Out

Question 7 is the one I couldn't answer with anything from class so far. A t-test needs a number you can average, and correlation needs two continuous variables. Question 7 only has two yes/no categories, so it needs a different kind of test altogether.
